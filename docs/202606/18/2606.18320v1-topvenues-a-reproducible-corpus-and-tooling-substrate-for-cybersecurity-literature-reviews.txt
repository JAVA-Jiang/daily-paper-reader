# TopVenues: A Reproducible Corpus and Tooling Substrate for Cybersecurity Literature Reviews 

Sidnei Barbieri 1,Ágney Lopes Roth Ferraz 1 and Lourenço Alves Pereira Júnior 11Divisão de Ciência da Computação – Instituto Tecnológico de Aeronáutica (ITA) Praça Marechal Eduardo Gomes, 50 – 12228-900 – São José dos Campos – SP – Brasil

Abstract. Cybersecurity literature reviews require a reproducible denomina-tor: the set of papers that a protocol includes before screening and synthesis begin. Today, that denominator is often reconstructed from publisher portals, bibliographic indices, and scholarly application programming interfaces (APIs) whose coverage, formats, and query semantics change over time. This paper presents TopVenues, an open-source system that materializes corpus construc-tion as a versioned research artifact. TopVenues declares a venue and year scope, uses DBLP Computer Science Bibliography (DBLP) as the metadata spine, enriches records with abstracts and BibTeX entries via open scholarly APIs and publisher-specific extractors, and stores the results in a monotonic SQLite snapshot, accessible via a command-line interface (CLI), a web interface, and export paths for review workflows. The May 2026 snapshot contains 9,925 papers from 11 cybersecurity sources over 2017 to 2026, with 99.86% abstract coverage and 99.99% BibTeX coverage; keyword search over the full corpus completes in under 31 ms, and a 250-test suite validates the data-integrity in-variants. The fixed denominator also enables repeatable measurement: 29.2% of 2024 to 2025 papers from the four top-ranked security conferences in our scope appear as arXiv preprints, with a median of five months before publica-tion, and a prior-author-track-record filter yields a 16.5 × precision gain at 90% recall for triaging preprints that later appear in the same venue set. TopVenues links corpus construction to auditable cybersecurity measurement by making the corpus itself executable, inspectable, and citable. The artifact is available at 

https://github.com/sidneibarbieri/topVenues .

## 1. Introduction 

Cybersecurity is one of the most prolific fields in computer science. The four security conferences classified in the A* tier, the highest tier, by the Computing Research and Education (CORE) ranking (USENIX Security, ACM CCS, IEEE S&P, and NDSS), account for nearly 6,000 papers in our corpus since 2019. Adding adjacent conferences and survey journals pushes the current review surface above 9,900 papers. For a researcher writing a survey, positioning a new system, or tracking an emerging topic, such as Large Language Model (LLM) security, the first scientific question is often blocked by a more basic one: What is the population of papers being reviewed? 

The usual answer is procedural, not reproducible. A researcher queries DBLP, ACM Digital Library, IEEE Xplore, USENIX, NDSS, publisher portals, and scholarly application programming interfaces (APIs), then reconciles results in a spreadsheet or

reference manager. This workflow can be documented, but it does not produce a stable denominator. Portal ranking changes, late DBLP updates, missing abstracts, rate limits, and inconsistent BibTeX exports all affect what is recovered. The resulting corpus is often treated as a temporary byproduct of the review rather than as a scientific object that another researcher can inspect, rerun, and extend. This denominator gap matters because literature reviews now serve a purpose beyond background sections. They drive research ideation, artifact positioning, technology monitoring, and meta-scientific measurements over the security community itself. System-atic literature review (SLR) methodology separates protocol design, search, selection, qual-ity assessment, extraction, and synthesis [ Kitchenham and Charters 2007 , Page et al. 2021 ,Rethlefsen et al. 2021 ]; screening tools such as Rayyan and ASReview improve collab-orative triage and prioritization [ Ouzzani et al. 2016 , van de Schoot et al. 2021 ]. These methods improve review discipline after a search process is specified, but they do not make the candidate set itself reproducible. Cybersecurity reviews highlight the importance of this missing layer. Surveys on intrusion detection and targeted attacks must reconcile heterogeneous venues, terminology, and evidence sources [ Khraisat et al. 2019 , Luh et al. 2017 ]. Cyber Threat Intelligence (CTI) work spans academic papers, vendor reports, public feeds, and sharing com-munities [ Shu et al. 2018 , Li et al. 2019 , Bouwman et al. 2020 , Bouwman et al. 2022 ,Barbieri et al. 2025 ]. In these settings, the corpus is not a clerical detail; it is the boundary of the claim. A survey that silently changes its denominator changes the evidence available to the reader. A reproducible cybersecurity review, therefore, needs the same kind of explicit protocol state for corpus construction that SLR guidelines already require for search documentation, study selection, and synthesis. 

TopVenues addresses this missing layer by making the corpus a versioned artifact. It defines a configurable publication scope, builds a DBLP-backed metadata spine, enriches records with abstracts and BibTeX entries from multiple sources, and preserves the results as a monotonic SQLite snapshot. The snapshot is immediately usable through a command-line interface (CLI) and web interface, and its scientific property is the denominator: every count, query, export, and measurement is tied to a declared denominator that can be reproduced from the released artifact. This paper makes five contributions. It formulates corpus construction as a distinct reproducibility problem, presents the TopVenues architecture, describes the reproducibility controls that make the artifact practical to execute, and releases and characterizes a 9,925-paper cybersecurity corpus, and uses that corpus as a denominator for two measurements: a 29.2% arXiv early-signal rate and a 16.5 × precision gain from a scientific-readiness filter. The work falls under data management and cybersecurity systems, within the broader theme of security-oriented data management and measurement. 

## 2. Problem and Requirements 

We distinguish corpus construction from corpus use . Corpus construction assembles the population of papers that satisfy a declared scope: venues, years, paper classes, metadata completeness, and refresh policy. Corpus use begins only after that population exists: screening, annotation, synthesis, querying, exporting, and measurement. Most review tools operate in the second phase. TopVenues targets the first, where an implicit search procedure must be made explicit and reusable. This framing yields six requirements. The scope must be curated and machine-readable (R1) because venue selection determines the literature population and should be configured via versioned configuration rather than an unrecorded portal query. The meta-data path must rely on open sources whenever possible (R2), so reviewers can reproduce the collection logic without institutional credentials. The corpus must be abstract-complete enough for topic filtering and semantic triage (R3), since title-only search misses much of the evidence that review work depends on. Refreshes must be monotonic (R4): once an abstract or BibTeX entry has been recovered, a later null response from another source must not erase it. The corpus must export directly to the tools researchers already use (R5), including BibTeX, comma-separated values (CSV), and JavaScript Object Notation (JSON). Finally, the artifact must be installable, documented, and testable from a fresh clone (R6), so that the main claims can be validated without publisher subscriptions or live scholarly APIs. These requirements define a boundary condition for a research artifact. The system does not need to automate scholarly judgment; it must preserve the inputs to that judg-ment. A reviewer should be able to ask why a record is present, what source supplied its abstract, whether the same snapshot can be reconstructed, and which records remain outside abstract-aware analysis. Those questions are the difference between a search result and a reproducible denominator. 

## 3. Architecture 

TopVenues is organized around a simple principle: the corpus is the persistent scientific object, and every tool is either a constructor, validator, or consumer of that object. Figure 1 contrasts this model with the common manual workflow. In the ad hoc model, portal queries reconstruct a corpus each time, and the denominator remains entangled with search semantics, deduplication choices, and spreadsheet state. In TopVenues , the declared scope enters a controlled construction boundary: DBLP supplies the metadata spine, enrichment sources supply abstracts and BibTeX, and the monotonic SQLite snapshot becomes the object that review workflows and measurement studies consume. The construction pipeline has four stages, each of which can be executed indepen-dently for auditing or chained into a full refresh. The download stage fetches DBLP JSON records for each configured source and year range, caching them in a per-source, per-year layout so that incremental runs retrieve only missing proceedings. Consolidation then normalizes source names, deduplicates records by DBLP key, and writes them to SQLite. The central invariant is monotonicity: for fields enriched after the DBLP import, such as abstracts and BibTeX, the database preserves the existing non-null value whenever a later run returns null. This makes enrichment regression a violation of the storage layer, not a convention left to individual call sites. The extraction stage fills the fields that DBLP does not provide. For each record missing an abstract, TopVenues queries a priority-ordered registry of open scholarly APIs and publisher-specific extractors. The first valid abstract is written back with provenance, and the remaining extractors for that record are skipped. This design keeps citation export usable even when upstream metadata is incomplete. In the released snapshot, the combined path yields 9,924 non-empty BibTeX entries out of 9,925 records. SQLite is the sole persistent store. This design serves R6 directly: the database is a single file that can be distributed as a research artifact, committed to a repository, shared via a download link, or packaged in a container. There is no external database server to configure, no credentials to manage, and no migration step required beyond a thin schema-evolution helper invoked on each startup. The monotonic merge is a storage-layer property, not an application convention. It combines write-with-replace semantics with a null-preserving coalesce over every enrichable field, so even application code that bypasses the high-level API cannot regress previously enriched data. A compressed-snapshot bootstrap mechanism complements the database. The repository ships a 15 MB gzip-compressed dump of the full SQLite database (74 MB uncompressed). When the database manager initializes and finds no local materialized database, it transparently decompresses the snapshot into place; the decompression takes under 1 second on a standard laptop. A small lineage sidecar records size and modification-time fingerprints at synchronization time, so a later upstream update is detected and auto-applied for users who have not modified the local database, while users with local modifications receive an explicit warning before any overwrite occurs. This allows review-ers to use the corpus immediately without running the full pipeline, satisfying R6 with a single, committed binary artifact rather than a multi-megabyte CSV. Abstract extraction uses a registry of interchangeable source adapters. Each adapter returns either an abstract or no result; the orchestrator holds an ordered list of adapters per venue type, and invokes them until a result is obtained. The current set covers Semantic Scholar, OpenAlex, CrossRef, ACM Digital Library, IEEE Xplore, USENIX, and NDSS. A new source becomes available with a single adapter and no changes to the or-chestrator or the database, satisfying R2 by making it easy to add open-source components as they appear and to replace sources that become unavailable or rate-limited. The command-line interface, installed as the topvenues executable, provides full pipeline control ( download , consolidate , extract , bibtex , run-all ) and cor-pus operations ( search , export , stats ). All commands accept -base-dir to point to a non-default data directory, enabling use in continuous integration (CI) pipelines and scripted workflows. This CLI is the primary interface for automation and reproducibility: all operations are deterministic when the database state is the same. The Streamlit interface ( topvenues web ) provides an interactive search with fil-ters for venue, year range, author, and keyword, along with per-venue coverage dashboards, topic distribution charts, and one-click BibTeX and CSV exports. It adds no functionality beyond the CLI, since every search and export ultimately issues the same database queries. 

## 4. Reproducibility Controls, Corpus Scope and Coverage 

The implementation focuses on reproducibility controls at the points where corpus drift typically occurs. Enrichment is the costly stage because it calls several scholarly APIs, so it runs concurrently over configurable batches and checkpoints each committed batch; an interrupted run resumes without reprocessing records it has already enriched. Every record is a typed value object validated against the database schema before any write reaches storage, so whitespace-only or boilerplate abstracts never enter the corpus. The review scope, comprising venues, year ranges, and extractor priorities, lives in a versioned 

config.yaml . Widening the corpus to a new venue is therefore a localized configuration change, which satisfies R1 by keeping scope changes auditable. The tests enforce the same discipline. The suite builds small SQLite databases in temporary directories and runs offline in under a second. Its integration tests exercise the monotonic upsert by inserting a record with an abstract, replaying a consolidation that returns null for that field, and asserting that the original abstract survives, validating R4, which requires it to hold. A compressed SQLite snapshot ships with the repository and materializes on first run, allowing a reviewer to reproduce the corpus state without credentials or network access. The current corpus covers 11 venues selected to represent the top tier of cyberse-curity research: the four CORE A* venues (USENIX Security, ACM CCS, IEEE S&P, NDSS) [ CORE 2023 ]; four additional conference venues (ACM ASIA CCS, IEEE Eu-roS&P, ACM SACMAT, HotNets); and three high-impact journals (ACM Computing Surveys, IEEE Communications Surveys & Tutorials, Foundations and Trends in Privacy and Security). The configured review window spans 2017 to 2026 in the current snapshot, with the two pre-2019 records retained because they appear on the DBLP venue pages included in the artifact. Abstract coverage is at or above 99.3% across all major venues; the single outlier is Foundations and Trends in Privacy and Security (8 papers total, 7 with abstracts), which publishes long monograph-style papers that are rarely indexed by open abstract sources. ## 5. Evaluation and Scientific Results 

We evaluate TopVenues against the six requirements in Section 2 using measurements taken on the released artifact, without network access, on an Apple M4 Max workstation with 64 GB of memory, Darwin 25.4.0, and Python 3.14.5. The evaluation has two layers. The first establishes artifact quality: coverage, latency, exportability, monotonic updates, and tests. The second asks whether the artifact enables a result that the community could not reliably obtain from ad hoc portal searches: early detection of top-tier cybersecurity research on arXiv. Together, these layers establish both practical utility and scientific yield: the same declared corpus that accelerates review work also becomes an auditable denominator for cybersecurity measurement. The corpus achieves 99.86% abstract coverage and 99.99% BibTeX coverage (Table 1). The 14 records with missing abstracts are distributed across seven venues, with six in IEEE Communications Surveys. They retain complete title, author, venue, year, DOI or source address, and BibTeX metadata, and are searchable by title; only abstract-based semantic filtering is unavailable for these 14 papers. The 9,910 records with both abstract and BibTeX fields provide the foundation for abstract-aware search and direct citation export. The remaining 15 records are still usable bibliographic records: one has an abstract but no BibTeX entry, and 14 have BibTeX but no abstract. This accounting makes the denominator explicit and prevents hard-to-enrich papers from being hidden from the corpus. abstract on the full 9,925-row table, without a full-text index, and materializes the complete result set. Latency is measured from issuing the SQLite query to receiving all result rows. All queries are completed in under 31 ms, which is imperceptible to an interactive user. Adding a CREATE INDEX ON papers(title, abstract)

The next three workflows illustrate how the same fixed corpus supports common cybersecurity research tasks without changing the denominator. In an intrusion-detection survey, a researcher issues a topic-specific query over the full corpus. The query returns 120 papers spanning 2019 to 2026 across 10 venues in 28 ms. Refining the filter to conference venues over the 2021 to 2026 window leaves 73 papers, each with title, authors, venue, year, and abstract; a BibTeX export of those 73 entries serializes in under 20 ms. The path from a fresh corpus bootstrap to a ready-to-import .bib file is shorter than five minutes on a laptop with no network access after the initial artifact download. For Cyber Threat Intelligence (CTI) methodology, a researcher issues a query against the same corpus and obtains 36 papers in 29 ms, distributed across ACM CCS (12), USENIX Security (7), ACM Computing Surveys (4), NDSS (4), IEEE Communications Surveys (3), IEEE EuroS&P (3), ACM ASIA CCS (2), and ACM SACMAT (1). The distribution immediately reveals that CTI work appears prominently in surveys, a structural finding that a manual multi-portal workflow would surface only after querying each venue separately and reconciling the results. For Large Language Model (LLM) security, a keyword query returns 288 papers. Their temporal distribution, one paper in 2021, ten in 2023, 73 in 2024, 152 in 2025, and 52 in the partial 2026 window, quantifies the field’s growth and suggests the appropriate review horizon, without any manual counting across portals. The measured claim is deliberately narrow and reproducible: after the snapshot is available, each case study above resolves the same corpus, counts, and exports from a fixed local database in interactive time. A controlled human-time speedup claim would require a user study across researchers, portals, and topics. A manual workflow can reach the same scholarly goal, but it must separately query venue portals, reconcile duplicates, normalize citations, and document omissions. TopVenues removes that source of denominator drift while leaving screening and synthesis to the researcher. The final case study treats the corpus as a measurement substrate : a stable, declared population against which an external signal can be correlated reproducibly. We ask a question that a manual multi-portal workflow cannot answer with a fixed denominator: 

how often, and how far in advance, does top-tier cybersecurity work appear as an arXiv preprint before its formal publication? 

We take the corpus cohort of 2,537 papers from the four CORE A* venues published in 2024 to 2025 and match each against a harvested snapshot of 27,749 arXiv preprints in the Computer Science, Cryptography and Security category (cs.CR) spanning 2022 to 2026, collected through the public arXiv API [ arXiv 2024 ]. A preprint matches a paper when at least one author key coincides, using last name plus first initial after accent and DBLP suffix normalization, and when the normalized-token Jaccard similarity between titles is at least 0.55. Here, Jaccard similarity is the fraction of shared normalized title tokens over the union of title tokens. Both thresholds deliberately favor false negatives, so the reported rate is a conservative lower bound. The preprint-to-publication lag is anchored to the first day of each venue’s conference month (USENIX Security August, ACM CCS October, IEEE S&P May, NDSS February) rather than January 1, which would otherwise create spurious negative lags for papers preprinted earlier in the publication year. Table 3 reports the outcome: 742 of 2,537 papers (29.2%) have a matching preprint, with a stable rate across the four venues (28.4% to 31.1%). The preprint precedes the conference by a median of 154 days (roughly 5 months), with a 25th-to-90th percentile range of 47 to 442 days. The few negative lags correspond to camera-ready versions posted shortly after acceptance, not to measurement error. A 29.2% coverage rate indicates that top-tier work appears early, but not which of the ∼7,000 annual cs.CR preprints are worth watching. We therefore test a triage filter: does authorship by a researcher with a prior publication in one of the four CORE A* security venues predict that a preprint will later appear in that same four-venue scope? Together, they show that the corpus can support reproducible literature monitoring: nearly a third of top-tier cybersecurity work is available on arXiv, with a median of 5 months ahead of publication, and a tunable author track-record filter prioritizes which preprints to inspect first. ## 6. Discussion 

The 11-venue list reflects a scope decision, informed by CORE rankings [ CORE 2023 ], rather than a claim of universal security coverage. The implementation represents scope as configuration, so later releases can publish alternative venue profiles without changing the construction logic. Enrichment is provenance-bearing metadata with recorded source paths. The snapshot records the source path for each abstract, uses public scholarly interfaces in the released pipeline, acknowledges the use of arXiv data in the preprint study, and keeps hard-to-enrich records visible rather than silently dropping them. The 14 missing abstract records, therefore, form a transparent coverage boundary. The monotonic COALESCE invariant guards only the bulk re-run path, where a later null response must not erase a recovered abstract. A record found to contain an incorrect or low-quality abstract is corrected through an explicit, timestamped field-level update, which also serves as the path for a correction or takedown request. Each release is a single compressed file identified by a Secure Hash Algorithm 256-bit (SHA-256) digest, so a reader can confirm an identical corpus state before reproducing any measurement. The corpus separates two kinds of stability that are often conflated in literature review tooling. The first is denominator stability: the set of records in a released snapshot must remain fixed so that a review or measurement can be cited, rerun, and compared later. The second is metadata quality: abstracts, source web addresses, and BibTeX entries can improve as upstream sources change or as users report defects. TopVenues handles this tension by treating the snapshot as immutable evidence and the construction pipeline as a repeatable process. A published snapshot is never silently rewritten; a later release can replace a lower-quality field with one with a better provenance path, but the change is visible in the database digest, the lineage metadata, and the release notes. This policy preserves the value of monotonic enrichment for bulk refreshes while still allowing explicit corrections when a field is wrong, truncated, or associated with the wrong paper. This separation is the paper’s core design insight. A literature review involves at least three decisions, often collapsed into a single spreadsheet: which population is eligible, which records are relevant to the research question, and which evidence the authors synthesize from those records. TopVenues does not automate the last two decisions. Instead, it makes the first one a reproducible object with a name, a scope, a digest, and provenance. That boundary is valuable because it localizes disagreement. If two reviewers disagree about whether a paper belongs in an intrusion-detection survey, they can now determine whether their disagreement concerns the declared population or the paper’s topical relevance within that population. Without that separation, both disputes are mixed with portal drift, missing abstracts, duplicate records, and citation-cleanup choices. The same model supports responsible redistribution. The artifact stores biblio-graphic metadata, abstracts recovered from scholarly metadata sources, source provenance, and links back to canonical publisher or venue pages. It does not claim ownership over the paper text, does not redistribute full papers, and keeps the provenance needed to remove or replace a field if a source policy or takedown request requires it. This makes the corpus useful as an executable research object while keeping the data-management boundary clear: the snapshot is a reproducible index and measurement substrate, not a substitute for the publisher record. The released README therefore describes both the execution and correction paths, so artifact evaluators can reproduce the claims and maintainers can keep future releases auditable. The submission snapshot and the living tool serve different scientific roles. The May 2026 snapshot serves as the denominator for all numbers in this paper; subsequent data refreshes may add newly indexed papers, improve abstracts, or broaden the venue profile, but they must be designated as new releases before they can be used to support a new measurement. This distinction prevents freshness from becoming an uncontrolled variable. A researcher who wants the latest corpus can update the artifact, while a reader who wants to verify this paper can replay the frozen snapshot and obtain the same counts. Treating updates as versioned releases also turns future changes into auditable deltas: added papers, corrected abstracts, and altered scope profiles can be inspected without changing the evidence base behind a published claim. This release discipline is what lets 

TopVenues be both a usable tool and a reproducible measurement substrate. SQLite is a deliberate fit for the current artifact because the primary workload is single-user, read-heavy, and snapshot-oriented. At 9,925 records, substring search over title and abstract returns results in tens of milliseconds, full-corpus exports complete interactively, and the compressed database remains small enough to version and mirror. The design also has a clear scale boundary. If the default scope grows to hundreds of thousands of papers, the same schema can add SQLite Full-Text Search (FTS5) indexes without changing the corpus model; if the workload changes from reproducible snapshot analysis to multi-user concurrent writes, the storage adapter can move to a server database while preserving the DBLP key, provenance fields, and monotonic field-update invariant. The contribution is therefore not tied to SQLite as a technology choice. SQLite is the smallest backend, making the artifact easy to distribute and reproduce today, while the persistent abstraction is the declared corpus, field-level provenance, and reproducible construction logic. The main scientific effect of TopVenues is that it turns every reported count into a statement over an explicit population. A keyword result, such as 36 CTI papers, is a count across the 9,925 records in the released snapshot, for the configured venues and years, with the 14 missing abstracts still visible. The same interpretation applies to the 742 arXiv matches and to the readiness-filter operating points: the reader can inspect the papers that entered the denominator, the preprints that matched it, and the records that the filter excluded. This property reduces the ambiguity that often makes measurements in the literature hard to compare. Two review teams may disagree about which papers are relevant to a topic, but the disagreement now starts from a shared input instead of two independently reconstructed search results. This property also explains why the measurements in Section 5 are not merely demonstrations of a tool interface. The intrusion-detection, CTI, and LLM-security exam-ples show that a fixed denominator makes ordinary review questions auditable: the reader can inspect the same 120, 36, or 288 records, rerun the same filters, and export the same BibTeX. The arXiv study then uses the same denominator as a measurement substrate: the published population is fixed first, and the external preprint signal is then compared against it. That order matters. If the population is reconstructed while the signal is being measured, source drift can masquerade as a scientific trend. By freezing the population before measuring the signal, TopVenues turns a monitoring question into a repeatable computation over a declared corpus. This contract also bounds the paper’s claims. The artifact supports the claims that the released scope is reconstructable, that the metadata fields have recorded provenance, that the enrichment path is monotonic under bulk refreshes, and that the measurements regenerate from the released scripts and snapshots. Claims about complete field coverage, publisher-canonical abstract text, and causal effects of prior top-4 authorship remain outside that boundary. This distinction is important for cybersecurity, where review work often mixes academic publications, operational reports, vendor material, and fast-moving preprints. A fixed corpus cannot remove the need for judgment, but it makes the boundary of that judgment inspectable. The contribution of TopVenues is not a new ranking or screening model, but a reproducible substrate for such methods: a declared denominator, monotonic construction process, and executable measurements tied to a versioned snapshot. Implementation choices such as SQLite, COALESCE-based updates, and SHA-256 digests matter only insofar as they preserve portability, provenance, and reproducibility. 

## 7. Threats to Validity 

Internal validity is bounded by the measurement environment. We measured latency in Section 5 on a single machine; actual latency will vary with hardware. Each reported latency is the median of five runs. The SQLite storage engine’s page cache may warm during repeated queries, slightly reducing apparent latency for later queries in a session. External validity is limited by the declared scope and the difficulty of directly comparing human review workflows. The time estimates for manual corpus construction were intentionally not reported as quantitative results because they depend on the researcher, the portal, the topic, and the amount of citation cleanup required. We report only measured artifact operations. The qualitative manual workflow comparison should therefore be read as a source-of-variance analysis rather than a human-time benchmark. Construct validity depends on how metadata completeness and preprint matching are operationalized. Abstract coverage is measured as the fraction of records with a non-empty abstract field. A small number of abstracts are reconstructed from PDF text instead of publisher-canonical metadata, so the artifact records its provenance and keeps the extraction path auditable. The released validation checks for non-empty abstracts and a minimum length of 20 characters; stronger source-level audits can run on the same snapshot without changing the corpus-construction model. The 29.2% preprint rate in Case Study D is a conservative lower bound by construc-tion: the author-key and title-similarity thresholds favor false negatives, so genuine matches with heavily reworded titles or first-author name changes are missed. On the precision side, the accepted matches are dominated by exact titles: 94% match the published title exactly, and 99% clear a Jaccard similarity threshold of 0.7, with the few false positives concentrated in the residual sub-0.7 band, such as distinct fake-image-detection papers that share an author. We harvest only the cs.CR arXiv category; papers preprinted solely in adjacent categories, such as Computer Science Machine Learning (cs. LG) or Computer Science Computers and Society (cs. CY), are not counted, which further depresses the rate. The lead-time anchor is the first day of each venue’s conference month, an approximation accurate to within a few weeks of the actual presentation date; it is used only for the lag distribution, not for the match rate, so the headline percentage is unaffected. The 16.5 × lift measures the precision of a practical filter , not a causal claim that track record causes acceptance. Part of the mechanism is that established authors continue to publish in top venues. The conversion outcome is detected by title similarity against the corpus, which can miss a preprint whose title was substantially rewritten before publication, so both precision and recall are lower bounds. Because the outcome window ends in 2026, preprints that become top-4 papers later are not yet counted, biasing the measured conversion rates downward , most visibly for the older 2022 cohort. 

## 8. Related Work 

Scholarly metadata and snapshot systems provide the raw material for TopVenues , but not the same research object. DBLP is the closest fit for a stable computer-science bibliog-raphy: it exposes venue pages, persistent keys, and structured metadata through public interfaces [ Ley 2009 , Ley 2002 ]. Semantic Scholar, OpenAlex, the Semantic Scholar Open Research Corpus (S2ORC), CrossRef, and publisher APIs provide broader meta-data and, in many cases, abstracts or citation links [ Ammar et al. 2018 , Priem et al. 2022 ,Lo et al. 2020 ]. TopVenues uses these systems as upstream sources in a controlled con-struction process. Their design goal is broad scholarly discovery or large-scale graph construction, whereas ours is a declared cybersecurity denominator that can be cited, inspected, and recomputed. This distinction matters for a venue-bounded review: a fresh query to a broad index may improve over time, but the improvement changes the population unless the slice, omissions, and provenance are captured in a versioned artifact. Broad scholarly graphs serve as upstream evidence sources for TopVenues . They help recover metadata, but they do not by themselves define which security venues, years, paper classes, and enrichment decisions constitute the population of a review. Review tools and reporting standards address a different layer of the workflow. Reference managers such as Zotero [ Center for History and New Media 2006 ] organize citations after collection, and systems such as Rayyan [ Ouzzani et al. 2016 ] and AS-Review [ van de Schoot et al. 2021 ] help reviewers screen or prioritize a candidate set. Reporting standards describe how to make review protocols, screening decisions, and bibliometric analyses more transparent [ Kitchenham and Charters 2007 , Page et al. 2021 ,Rethlefsen et al. 2021 ]. Automation surveys and bibliometric guidance explain which review steps can be assisted by tools and which choices remain methodological [ Van Dinter et al. 2021 , Linnenluecke et al. 2020 ]. TopVenues sits before those tools. It makes the candidate population explicit enough that downstream relevance judgments have a stable input. This position is also why the artifact exports BibTeX, CSV, and JSON instead of replacing existing review environments. 

TopVenues therefore occupies a different layer than the screening and reporting tools used after a candidate set is created. A review protocol can say which databases and queries were used, and a screening tool can record inclusion decisions, but neither artifact necessarily preserves the exact population over which those decisions were made. This gap matters when the same research question is revisited months later, because changes in source coverage can appear to be changes in the field. The contribution of TopVenues is to make the candidate population itself an executable object before relevance judgments begin. That design makes later screening, synthesis, and disagreement more interpretable: the reviewer can separate a dispute about scope from a dispute about relevance. The distinction is especially important for cybersecurity because the field’s evidence base is heterogeneous. A review may combine top-conference papers, survey journals, arXiv preprints, vendor reports, public feeds, vulnerability databases, and code artifacts. Tools that optimize screening effort are valuable once the candidate set exists, but they do not decide whether a late DBLP update, a missing publisher abstract, a renamed venue page, or a cross-listed preprint changed the denominator. TopVenues contributes a narrower object: a citable population for scholarly literature, with enough provenance to explain what was included, what remained incomplete, and which measurements were computed over that population. Security reproducibility and data stewardship motivate the artifact boundary. Re-cent security work shows that the availability of artifacts and data remains difficult to evaluate in practice. Studies of machine-learning security papers measure gaps in code and dataset availability [ Olszewski et al. 2023 ], work on security data sharing shows that reusable datasets remain hard to locate and interpret [ Crowder et al. 2025 ], and a USENIX Security Systematization of Knowledge (SoK) argues that replicability requires explicit reasoning about the problem, domain, method, data, and analysis [ Olszewski et al. 2025 ]. 

TopVenues applies this artifact discipline to literature denominators. Its data-management boundary is deliberately narrow: it stores bibliographic metadata, abstracts, provenance, and reproducible measurements, while pointing back to canonical publisher or venue pages for the papers themselves. The Findable, Accessible, Interoperable, and Reusable (FAIR) principles provide the broader open-science frame for this choice [ Wilkinson et al. 2016 ]. 

## 9. Conclusion 

TopVenues turns corpus construction from a transient search procedure into a reproducible system boundary for cybersecurity literature reviews. The released artifact fixes the scope, records provenance, preserves enriched fields through a monotonic SQLite snapshot, and makes the same denominator available to search, export, testing, and measurement. The May 2026 snapshot provides near-complete abstract and BibTeX coverage across 9,925 papers and reproduces the arXiv early-signal and readiness-filter measurements without using live portals. This makes review quality auditable before screening. References
